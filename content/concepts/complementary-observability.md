What makes quantum-stuff interesting as a form of information is not superposition (being in two states at once), or entanglement (two states necessarily being the same even when physically separated), it is a less talked about characteristic sometimes called *complementary-observability* — although I think a better name is *complementary knowledge*, which is how I'll refer to it here.

Superposition is a common phenomena we see in many classical places in the world. For example when two notes are played at the same time, the two notes are in superposition in the final wave-form. You can tell because you can stop one wave and get back out the other one. You can even identify both notes if you work a little harder.

Complementary knowledge is where things truly get strange. The idea is that by measuring or knowing one thing, you make it impossible to also know another. It says that there are natural physical phenomena the state of which cannot be fully known. If you try to know the other thing by measuring it, you will lose your knowledge of the first — the first will literally become random by the act of measuring the second thing.

It's surprisingly hard to find real-world analogies for complementary observability, do you have any ideas?
